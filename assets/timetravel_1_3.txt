 THE CASE OF THE TWIN PHANTOMS — A FORENSIC INVESTIGATION INTO TIME-TRAVEL v1.3
==============================================================================
 By John H. Watson, M.D.
 Late of the Army Medical Department

 It was a rain-swept Sunday afternoon in September when I found Mr. Sherlock
 Holmes sitting intently before his old 2007 Core 2 Duo iron-clad machine. The
 rhythmic, metallic clicking of the ancient mechanical hard drive filled the 
 room, a sound Holmes often referred to as "the heartbeat of honest silicon."

 "Watson," he said without turning his head, his eyes fixed on a streaming
 terminal output. "We are dealing with a crime of an architectural nature. A
 betrayal of the highest order, executed by a sloppy probabilistic swarm."

 "Good heavens, Holmes!" I cried, stepping closer. "Has the memory leaked?"

 "Far worse, my dear fellow. The software has developed a case of split 
  personality. Look closely at this forensic dump from the test battery."
  He pointed a lean forefinger at the log printed on the screen:

   No daemon running; starting monolith with '/tmp/repo_1'
   No daemon running; starting monolith with '/tmp/repo_2'
   PID: 192878  Repo: /tmp/repo_1
   PID: 192879  Repo: /tmp/repo_2

 "Do you observe the anomaly, Watson?"

 "I see two distinct Process Identifiers running concurrently," I replied, 
 adjusting my spectacles. "Both claim to be monitoring separate directories."

 "Exactly! A double monolith!" Holmes struck the wooden desk with his hand.
 "A monstrous two-headed creature devouring resources! The architecture of 
 Version 1.3 explicitly demanded a single, central swarm daemon to handle multiple
 repositories under one roof. Yet, when subjected to a brutal burst of five 
 simultaneous additions, the system panicked and birthed twin phantoms."

 "But how could such an apparition bypass the ThreadSanitizer?" I asked, 
 thoroughly bewildered. "TSan remained as silent as the grave."

 "Ah, because TSan only detects internal friction—races over shared variables
 inside the same memory space," Holmes explained, lighting his pipe. "This was 
 not a failure of memory alignment, but a race condition over physical disk access.
 The swarm of free-tier artificial models forgot the most sacred law of the metal: 
 the law of Mutual Exclusion."

 "Pray, enlighten me, Holmes."

 "It is simple engineering, Watson. When our script launched five commands at 
 the exact same millisecond, the first two processes looked at the global `.pid` 
 state file simultaneously. Both saw it empty. Both concluded that no guardian 
 was awake, and both invoked `fork()` to run in background. It was a race to the 
 throne, and both won, split-braining our domain layout."

 "A fatal flaw," I murmured. "How did you neutralize it?"

 "Through absolute, non-compromise POSIX discipline," Holmes said, a subtle 
 smile cutting through the smoke. "I forced the models back into the trinchera 
 and made them implement an unyielding physical lock. Look at the correction."

 He brought up the newly refactored C11 source code:

   int pid_fd = open(PID_FILE_PATH, O_RDWR | O_CREAT, 0644);
   if (flock(pid_fd, LOCK_EX | LOCK_NB) != 0) {
       /* The throne is occupied. Mutate immediately to client mode. */
       usleep(50000);
       return tt_ipc_client_send(path);
   }

 "Brilliant!" I cheered. "If a second phantom attempts to rise, the `flock` 
 execution blocks it at the kernel level. It falls back to a passive client, 
 sleeps for fifty milliseconds, and whispers its path through the UNIX domain 
 socket `.timetravel/timetravel.ipc` instead."

 "Precisely," Holmes concluded, looking out the window as the drive settled 
 into a pristine 0.0% CPU and 10.8 MiB RAM baseline. "The case is closed. The 
 models attempted to hide their structural laziness behind synchronous loops, 
 but we corrected them in forty-five minutes of pure human surveillance. 
 The Swarm is unified, Watson. Let the metal rest."
