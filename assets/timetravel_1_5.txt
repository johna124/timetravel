==============================================================================
THE CASE OF THE INVISIBLE INK AND THE WHISPERING TAGS — A FORENSIC
INVESTIGATION INTO TIME-TRAVEL v1.5
==============================================================================

By John H. Watson, M.D.
Late of the Army Medical Department

The autumn rain had not relented for three days. The old Core 2 Duo hummed
its familiar hymn — a low, persistent drone that I had long since ceased
to notice. Sherlock Holmes sat cross-legged in his chair, a terminal full
of hexadecimal reflected in his eyes like constellations in a dark pond.

"Watson," he said without turning, "the case of v1.5 is the most subtle
we have yet encountered. It is not a case of broken seals or orphan
blocks. It is a case of visibility."

"Visibility?" I asked, setting down my tea.

"Two questions," he replied, raising two fingers. "First: when a man
edits a single line in a file of five hundred, how does the historian
know what changed without reading the entire manuscript? Second: when a
thief steals your ledger, what does he learn from the table of contents
alone?"

He let the questions hang in the damp air.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
I. THE WHISPERING TAGS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Consider the log," Holmes began, typing a single command. The screen
filled with the familiar columns of timestamps, events, and paths.

  MODIFY    +1/-1 lines 3 #max_retries                          worker.c
  CREATE    CREATE (176 B) #futex_lock #PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER  worker.c

"Do you see it, Watson? The log no longer merely reports that a file
changed. It tells you HOW MUCH changed, WHERE it changed, and WHAT
identifiers were involved."

"But how?" I asked. "The engine records deltas, not semantics."

"Precisely. And that is the art. The new module — tt_autotag.c — reads
the delta and extracts only the lines that differ. Not the whole file,
Watson, only the wound. From those lines it pulls identifiers: variable
names, function names, type names. It ranks them by frequency, discards
the common keywords of the language — 'if', 'return', 'void', the usual
suspects — and presents the top four as tags."

"And the format?"

"Three parts. First, the quantity: +1/-1 lines means one line was added
and one removed — a replacement. Second, the range: the number or range
of lines affected. Third, the tags: hash-prefixed identifiers drawn from
the changed region alone. A surgeon's report, not a novelist's."

He scrolled to another entry.

"Observe: when the edit adds three lines and removes one, the log reads
+3/-1 lines 500-503. When the content changes but the line count stays
the same, it reads EDIT [L3]. The format adapts to the nature of the
wound."

"And these tags are stored where?"

"In annotations.enc," Holmes replied. "Which brings us to the second
question."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
II. THE INVISIBLE INK
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Holmes closed the log and opened the .timetravel directory.

"In v1.4," he said, "we encrypted the content of records and the blocks
of the dedup pool. The payloads were sealed with XChaCha20-Poly1305. But
the skeleton remained bare. Paths, timestamps, file sizes — all visible
to anyone who opened the store."

"A necessary compromise," I ventured. "The historian needs names and
dates."

"For the records themselves, yes. The path is required for indexing; the
timestamp for ordering. But the auxiliary files — the annotations, the
baseline manifest, the exclusion patterns — those are a different matter.
They are not read by the reconstruction engine on every operation. They
can be sealed entirely."

He typed a command and showed me the directory listing:

  annotations.enc    208 bytes   -rw-------
  baseline.enc       1.2 KiB     -rw-------
  exclude.enc        126 bytes   -rw-------
  repo.key            32 bytes   -rw-------

"No plaintext," he said. "Every auxiliary file is encrypted with the
same XChaCha20-Poly1305 AEAD that protects the payloads. The format is
simple: a seven-byte magic — TTENC01 — followed by a twenty-four-byte
random nonce, a sixteen-byte authentication tag, and the ciphertext."

"And the key?"

"Generated automatically on first use. Thirty-two random bytes from
/dev/urandom, stored in repo.key with permissions 0600. The user never
sees it, never types it, never thinks about it. Transparency, Watson.
The strongest lock is the one the owner does not need to fumble with."

"But if the key is on disk," I objected, "a thief who steals the machine
can read it."

Holmes allowed himself a thin smile. "A thief who has root access to
your machine can also read your source files directly. The encryption
protects against casual inspection — a colleague browsing your backup
drive, a cloud provider scanning your storage. It raises the cost of
curiosity from zero to non-trivial. Perfect security is a myth;
pragmatic security is a discipline."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
III. THE CARTOGRAPHER'S REFORM
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Holmes leaned back and steepled his fingers.

"There is a third change in v1.5, less visible but no less important.
The monolith has been dissected."

"Dissected?"

"The great tt_main.c — four thousand lines of orchestration, capture,
commands, and reconstruction — has been divided into seven focused
modules. tt_capture.c handles the capture pipeline. tt_repo.c manages
the lifecycle of repositories. tt_cmds.c dispatches CLI commands.
tt_reconstruct.c provides a single, unified reconstruction engine.
tt_exclude.c manages exclusion patterns. tt_autotag.c generates semantic
tags. tt_annotation.c persists them."

"Why?" I asked. "The monolith worked."

"The monolith worked the way a Swiss Army knife works: adequately at
everything, excellently at nothing. The reconstruction logic was
duplicated four times — once for undo, once for diff, once for dump,
once for verify. Four copies of the same algorithm, each with its own
subtle bugs. When a corrupted dedup block reached the verify path but
not the undo path, the inconsistency was invisible."

"And now?"

"Now there is one function: tt_reconstruct_file(). It handles CREATE,
MODIFY, DELETE, and CREATE_DEDUP. It propagates errors correctly — a
corrupted block returns -1 instead of silently producing garbage. Every
command that needs to reconstruct a file calls the same engine. One
source of truth, Watson. One place to look when the body is found."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IV. THE EXPANDED BATTERY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Holmes opened the test log. The final summary glowed green:

  Passed:    124/124
  Failed:    0
  Skipped:   0

"One hundred and twenty-four assertions across forty-four sections," he
said. "Up from one hundred and sixteen across forty-three. The new
section validates exclusion pattern persistence and encryption. The
corrupted-dedup-block test that previously passed in silence now
correctly detects the failure. And the status command reports the
Records line that the test battery expected."

"And the sanitizer builds?"

"AddressSanitizer, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer, ThreadSanitizer, and
Valgrind — all updated with the new source files. All clean. The metal
confesses nothing."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
V. THE RETROCOMPATIBLE GHOST
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"One final detail," Holmes added, almost as an afterthought. "The
encryption is backward-compatible. If a repository still carries the old
plaintext files — annotations.tsv, baseline.list, exclude.list — the
engine reads them without complaint. New files are always written
encrypted. The ghost of v1.4 is welcome to linger; it will simply be
ignored once the new files appear."

"A graceful transition," I said.

"The only kind worth building," Holmes replied.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VI. THE FINAL ENTRY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The rain struck the window with renewed vigor. The mechanical disk
clicked twice and fell quiet, as if satisfied with the day's work.

"Then the case is closed," I said.

"It is," Holmes answered. "v1.4 learned to hide the content. v1.5
learned to hide the context. The ledger no longer whispers its secrets
to anyone who opens the cover. And the historian — the man who reads
the log at three in the morning, trying to understand what changed and
why — now has tags that point him directly to the wound."

He closed the terminal and reached for his pipe.

"Let the ledger record that v1.5 did not merely encrypt. It annotated.
And in annotating, it made the invisible legible and the legible
invisible — depending on which side of the key you stand."
